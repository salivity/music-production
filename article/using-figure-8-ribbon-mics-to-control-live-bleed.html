Using Figure-8 Ribbon Mics to Control Live Bleed

Ribbon microphones utilize a bidirectional, or figure-8, polar pattern that provides engineers with an exceptional tool for controlling stage bleed during live performances. While traditionally reserved for the studio, ribbon mics excel in live production because their physical design creates extreme rejection zones along the sides of the capsule. By strategically orienting these deep acoustic nulls toward neighboring sound sources, audio engineers can isolate instruments with surgical precision, resulting in cleaner front-of-house mixes and higher gain-before-feedback.

The Power of Side-Null Rejection

Unlike cardioid microphones, which offer maximum rejection at the rear (180 degrees off-axis), a figure-8 polar pattern exhibits deafening rejection at its sides (90 and 270 degrees off-axis). In practice, these side null points can offer 30 dB or more of attenuation across a broad frequency spectrum. In a live environment where loud sources are arranged side-by-side—such as a guitar amplifier next to a bass rig, or a hi-hat positioned inches from a snare drum—aiming the physical side of a ribbon microphone directly at the adjacent noise source completely eliminates the spill that would typically overwhelm a cardioid microphone's wider pickup pattern.

Managing the Rear Lobe

Because a figure-8 microphone picks up sound equally from both the front and the back, utilizing one live requires conscious management of the rear lobe. Live engineers mitigate unwanted rear pickup using several reliable techniques:

  • Stage Monitor Placement: Floor wedges should never be placed directly behind a figure-8 mic. Instead, wedge monitors must be positioned in the side nulls (roughly 90 degrees to the capsule) to avoid severe feedback loops. In-ear monitoring (IEM) setups eliminate this concern entirely, making figure-8 mics far easier to deploy.
  • Vertical Angling: When miking instruments like acoustic guitars, percussion, or brass, angling the microphone downward places the rear lobe facing up toward acoustic treatment or empty ceiling space, rather than toward reflecting backlines or drum kits.
  • Gobo and Amp Baffling: When isolating guitar cabinets, the rear of the ribbon mic can be aimed into an isolation shield, an acoustic baffle, or a dead space on stage, ensuring that only the desired speaker cone is captured cleanly by the front lobe.

Natural Off-Axis Tone

Even when acoustic bleed inevitably enters a microphone, ribbon designs handle that spill differently than traditional dynamic or condenser elements. Condenser and dynamic mics often suffer from severe off-axis phase coloration, resulting in brittle, unnatural bleed that muddies a live mix. Ribbon microphones maintain a remarkably consistent frequency response even as sound arrives off-axis. Any ambient bleed that enters the front or rear lobes sounds smooth and natural, preventing the harsh phase smearing common to multi-mic live stages.

Practical Live Applications

In practical live sound scenarios, figure-8 ribbon mics provide distinct operational advantages:

  • Drums: Placed horizontally under a snare or positioned on a hi-hat, the side nulls can be aimed directly at the cymbals to capture the direct transient without piercing cymbal wash.
  • Horns and Strings: In orchestral or horn sections where players stand in close proximity, ribbon mics can be aligned so that the side nulls point directly at adjacent players, maintaining discrete track separation.
  • Dual-Source Capture: A single figure-8 ribbon can deliberately capture two sound sources simultaneously—such as two backing vocalists facing each other—while completely rejecting sound from the drums and monitors located on either side.
