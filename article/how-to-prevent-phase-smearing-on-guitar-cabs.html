How to Prevent Phase Smearing on Guitar Cabs

Using multiple microphones on a guitar cabinet creates rich, complex tones, but arrival-time differences between mic capsules often cause phase smearing and comb filtering that hollow out your sound. To combat this, producers combine meticulous physical positioning, phase-correlation checks, complementary mic pairings, and digital alignment techniques. By understanding how sound waves hit each capsule, you can ensure your multi-mic guitar tracks remain punchy, full, and focused in the final mix.

Align Capsules Physically at the Source

The most effective way to eliminate phase issues is at the physical source. When sound waves travel different distances to reach different microphone capsules, the phase relationship between the recorded signals shifts.

To align capsules physically:

  • The Sight Line Method: Use a flashlight through the cabinet grille to locate the speaker cone and dust cap precisely. Position the diaphragms of both microphones equidistant from the grille cloth.
  • The Touching Capsule Approach (Fredman Technique): Place one primary microphone (often a dynamic like a Shure SM57) pointing directly at the speaker cone, and place a second identical microphone right next to it, angled 45 degrees off-axis with their capsules touching at the exact same physical plane. This captures tonal variation without timing discrepancies.

Apply the 3:1 Distance Rule

If you are using a room mic or secondary mic placed further back rather than a blended close-mic pair, apply the 3:1 rule of microphone placement. The distance between the secondary microphone and the primary microphone should be at least three times the distance between the primary microphone and the guitar cabinet. This level difference ensures that any delayed signal entering the second mic is quiet enough that phase cancellation remains inaudible.

Monitor in Mono and Invert Polarity

Stereo separation can mask phase problems during tracking. Always sum your monitor output to mono when adjusting mic positions.

  1. Bring up your primary microphone to a normal listening level.
  2. Unmute the second microphone and listen for any thinning in the low-end or a "hollowed-out" midrange.
  3. Invert the polarity (\(\varnothing\)) on the second channel. If the signal becomes significantly quieter or thin, the microphones are mostly in phase when the polarity switch is off. If flipping the polarity switch restores the low frequencies, leave it inverted, or nudge the physical microphone until the two signals reinforce each other.

Calibrate Using a Test Tone or White Noise

Human ears struggle to detect subtle phase cancellations across complex guitar riffs in real-time. To make alignment obvious:

  • Send a burst of pink noise or a steady test tone through the guitar amplifier.
  • Invert the polarity on the secondary microphone channel.
  • While listening in mono, move the physical microphone forward and backward in millimeter increments until you find the point of maximum silence (cancellation).
  • Flip the polarity switch back to normal. The two microphones are now perfectly in phase.

Nudge Waveforms and Use Alignment Plugins in the DAW

If minor phase smearing persists after recording, fix it in your digital audio workstation (DAW):

  • Manual Waveform Zoom: Zoom in to the sample level on both tracks. Identify the initial transient peak of a pick attack, and use the slip-edit tool to drag the secondary track until its transient peaks line up with the primary track.
  • Sample Delay: Insert a sample delay plugin on the earlier track to delay it by a few samples until the phase correlation meter reads as close to +1 as possible.
  • Dedicated Phase Plugins: Process the tracks using phase-alignment plugins such as Sound Radix Auto-Align, Waves InPhase, or MeldaProduction MAutoAlign to automatically calculate time offsets and apply micro-delays or phase rotation curves across the frequency spectrum.
