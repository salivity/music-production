What Is True Peak Detection in Mastering Limiters?

True peak detection in a mastering limiter identifies and controls inter-sample peaks that standard digital sample-peak meters fail to catch, preventing unintended clipping and distortion when digital audio is converted to analog or encoded into streaming formats. As modern music production heavily relies on lossy distribution formats like AAC and MP3 alongside normalization standards on platforms like Spotify and Apple Music, true peak detection ensures that maximized, loud masters retain their sonic integrity across all consumer playback systems.

Sample Peaks vs. True Peaks

Traditional digital meters measure sample peaks: individual numerical snapshots of an audio signal taken at a set sample rate (such as 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz). If consecutive samples sit close to 0 dBFS (decibels relative to full scale), the continuous analog waveform generated by a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) can peak significantly higher between those individual samples.

These occurrences are known as inter-sample peaks (ISPs). While a standard peak limiter may report a maximum ceiling of -0.1 dBFS, the actual analog signal can cross 0 dBFS by up to 1 to 3 dB, resulting in audible distortion, harshness, and transient smearing on playback hardware.

The Impact of Lossy Audio Encoding

Modern distribution relies heavily on lossy codecs such as MP3, Ogg Vorbis, and AAC. During encoding, the mathematical reduction of file size frequently shifts transient peaks upward.

If a master is limited using only standard peak metering right up to the digital ceiling, the encoding process will inevitably push those inter-sample peaks past 0 dBFS. This causes hard digital clipping during decoding, introducing harsh artifacts in the high-frequency range that degrade the listening experience.

How True Peak Detection Works

True peak limiters use oversampling—typically 4x or higher—to reconstruct the continuous analog waveform mathematically before applying gain reduction.

By modeling the digital-to-analog conversion process in real time according to standards like ITU-R BS.1770, the limiter accurately locates inter-sample peaks. It then applies gain reduction precisely where the reconstructed waveform would exceed the set ceiling, preventing clipping before the signal ever leaves the digital domain.

Modern Industry Standards and Best Practices

True peak measurement is a core requirement across both broadcast regulations and streaming delivery specifications:

  • Broadcast Compliance: Standards such as ITU-R BS.1770, EBU R128, and ATSC A/85 require audio to remain below specified True Peak levels (typically -1.0 dBTP) to avoid transmission and consumer device overload.
  • Streaming Delivery: Major platforms recommend mastering with true peak limiting enabled, often advising a ceiling between -1.0 dBTP and -2.0 dBTP for high-volume masters to provide sufficient headroom for lossy encoding.

The Role in Modern Workflows

Enabling true peak detection provides predictability. Rather than guessing whether a competitive, loud master will clip on consumer devices or degrade under codec compression, mastering engineers use true peak limiting as a reliable safety net. It allows producers to push for modern loudness and punch without compromising the technical compliance and auditory fidelity of the final release.
