How to Mic an Upright Bass for Jazz Recording

Capturing the natural, resonant, and dynamic sound of an upright acoustic bass in jazz music production requires a deliberate approach to microphone choice and placement. This guide details the industry-standard miking techniques used in modern and traditional jazz sessions, outlining microphone positioning, isolation strategies, and the practice of blending acoustic microphones with direct pickup signals to achieve a balanced, authentic jazz tone.

The Bridge and Fingerboard Approach (The Core Acoustic Tone)

The most common single-microphone technique involves placing a large-diaphragm condenser (LDC) or ribbon microphone 6 to 18 inches away from the instrument, aimed at the area between the bridge and the end of the fingerboard.

  • Placement: Position the capsule level with the bridge, angled slightly upward toward the fingerboard.
  • Tonal Result: This position captures the full body and low-end resonance from the top plate while preserving the finger attack, string definition, and fretless nuances essential to jazz bass lines.
  • Adjustments: Moving the microphone closer increases low-end weight via the proximity effect; moving it higher toward the fingerboard emphasizes percussive string clicks.

The F-Hole Technique (Low-End Weight)

When a mix demands more deep fundamental frequencies, engineers place a microphone near the bass-side (lower) F-hole.

  • Placement: Position a dynamic or condenser microphone 4 to 8 inches off-axis from the lower F-hole. Avoid pointing directly down the opening, as this produces a boomy, resonant buildup around 80–120 Hz.
  • Tonal Result: Heavy low-end response with prominent cabinet resonance, though often lacking the high-frequency snap of string articulation.
  • Application: This method works well in sparse trio formats or when blended with a secondary microphone aimed at the neck.

The Bridge-Mounted Capsule (Isolation in Live Ensembles)

In traditional jazz sessions where the band plays together in the same room, bleed from drums and piano is a major challenge. The bridge-mounted technique solves this by placing a microphone as close to the sound source as possible.

  • Placement: Wrap a small-diaphragm condenser (SDC) or an omnidirectional lavalier microphone in acoustic foam and gently wedge it directly between the tailpiece and the bridge, or beneath the bridge arch.
  • Tonal Result: High isolation and dry fundamental tone with virtually zero spill from the rest of the ensemble.
  • Trade-off: It sacrifices some natural acoustic air and room depth, which can be reintroduced later via studio reverb.

Figure-8 Ribbon Miking (Bleed Management)

In open-room jazz recording, ribbon microphones with bidirectional (figure-8) pickup patterns are favored for their smooth high-end roll-off and exceptional side rejection.

  • Placement: Place the ribbon microphone roughly 1 to 2 feet in front of the bass, positioning the completely dead null plane (the sides of the microphone) directly toward the drum kit or brass section.
  • Tonal Result: A warm, vintage tone reminiscent of classic Blue Note and Prestige recordings, paired with clean acoustic separation despite tracking in a shared space.

Blending Mic and Piezo DI (The Modern Jazz Approach)

Most working jazz bassists use an onboard piezo pickup. Combining the direct injection (DI) signal with an acoustic microphone creates a mix-ready sound with both transient clarity and organic body.

  • The Setup: Send the piezo pickup to a high-impedance DI box while placing an LDC or ribbon microphone on the instrument.
  • Phase Alignment: Because the acoustic sound takes several milliseconds to travel through the air to the microphone capsule while the electrical DI signal is instantaneous, zoom into the audio waveforms in your digital audio workstation (DAW) and manually align the peaks of the DI track with the microphone track.
  • Tonal Balance: Use the DI track to provide steady low-end punch and mid-range string articulation, and use the microphone track to deliver warmth, dimension, and acoustic realism.
