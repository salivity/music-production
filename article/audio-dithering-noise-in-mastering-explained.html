Audio Dithering Noise in Mastering Explained

Audio dithering is the process of adding a microscopic amount of randomized noise to an audio signal when reducing its bit depth during final mastering. This article explains how dithering functions, why it is essential for preventing harsh digital truncation distortion, how it preserves low-level details like reverb tails and fades, and the precise moment it should be applied in the music production workflow.

The Problem: Bit Depth Reduction and Truncation

Modern music is typically recorded, mixed, and processed at high resolutions, such as 32-bit floating point or 24-bit fixed point. These formats offer immense dynamic range and headroom. However, final distribution formats often require lower bit depths, such as 16-bit for CDs and standard streaming files.

When downsampling from a higher bit depth to a lower one, the system must discard the extra binary data. If you simply chop off the extra bits—a process called truncation—the digital system rounds audio sample values to the nearest available bit value.

This rounding is not random; it correlates directly with the music signal. This mathematical correlation produces quantization error, which manifests as harsh, gritty harmonic distortion. Quantization distortion is especially noticeable in quiet passages, the natural decay of instruments, reverb tails, and fade-outs.

The Function of Dithering Noise

Dithering solves quantization distortion by introducing a controlled, low-level randomized noise—known as dither noise—into the audio signal immediately before the bit depth is reduced.

The dither noise acts as an analog-style buffer that randomizes the rounding process. Instead of rounding numbers based strictly on where the audio waveform falls, the added noise fluctuates the values continuously. This accomplishes two crucial tasks:

  1. Decorrelation of Errors: Dither decorrelates the quantization error from the original musical signal. By breaking this mathematical relationship, the harsh, metallic distortion is converted into benign, steady, low-level analog-like background hiss.
  2. Preservation of Low-Level Information: Because the random noise causes values to toggle between the nearest available bits, the human ear and brain can average out these fluctuations over time. This psychoacoustic phenomenon allows listeners to perceive musical details that exist below the theoretical dynamic range floor of the lower bit depth format.

In effect, dithering trades a small, consistent, and largely imperceptible noise floor for the total elimination of jarring digital distortion.

Noise Shaping

Many modern mastering dither algorithms feature noise shaping. Noise shaping alters the frequency curve of the dither noise, shifting it out of the frequency range where the human ear is most sensitive (roughly 1 kHz to 5 kHz) and pushing it up into very high, less audible frequencies (typically above 12 kHz to 15 kHz). This makes the noise floor even less noticeable to the listener while retaining all the anti-distortion benefits of standard dither.

When to Apply Dither

Dither should only be applied under specific conditions:

  • Apply dither when downsampling bit depth: For example, when exporting a 32-bit float or 24-bit project to a 16-bit audio file.
  • Apply dither only once: Dither should be the absolute last process in the mastering chain. If you dither an audio file and then perform further digital processing (such as EQ, compression, or limiting), you recalculate the bit values and will need to dither again, resulting in an accumulated, unnecessary buildup of noise.
  • Do not dither when maintaining bit depth: If exporting a 24-bit mix to a 24-bit master, or if rendering to a 32-bit floating-point file for an archivist or another engineer, dithering is not required.
