How Modular Synths Process Control Voltage

Modular synthesizers manipulate sound primarily through the generation, routing, and processing of Control Voltage (CV), an analog electrical signal used to dictate parameters such as pitch, timbre, and volume. Unlike fixed-architecture synthesizers with hardwired connections, modular systems rely on patch cables to send these non-audible voltages into various functional units known as modules. By treating parameters as dynamic, alterable voltages rather than static values, artists can manipulate audio signals with precision, creating everything from structured melodic sequences to evolving, chaotic textures.

Types of Control Voltage Signals

To manipulate audio effectively, modular systems utilize three primary categories of CV, each serving a distinct structural role:

  • Continuous Modulation: Derived from Low-Frequency Oscillators (LFOs) or random voltage generators, these slow-moving signals continuously sweep parameters like filter cutoff frequencies or panning positions.
  • Time-Variant Envelopes: Generated by envelope generators (such as ADSR modules), these transient voltages rise and fall in response to a note event, typically shaping an amplifier’s volume contour or a filter’s brightness over time.
  • Discrete Triggers and Gates: Binary-style signals that indicate timing. A trigger is an instantaneous pulse used to fire drum sounds or advance sequencers, while a gate remains high for the duration of a key press to sustain an envelope.
  • Pitch CV: Standardized in many systems (such as Eurorack) to the 1 Volt Per Octave (1V/Oct) standard, where an increase of one volt raises an oscillator's frequency by exactly one octave.

Processing and Conditioning CV

Raw CV signals often require modification before they reach their destination, allowing synthesists to fine-tune how much influence one module has over another.

Attenuation, Inversion, and Offsetting

Modulation sources frequently output a wider voltage range than desired. Attenuators act as passive or active volume controls for CV, scaling down the amplitude of the control signal to achieve subtle modulations. Attenuverters expand this capability by allowing the signal to be inverted (turned upside down), meaning a rising voltage becomes a falling voltage. DC offset generators add or subtract a steady baseline voltage, shifting the entire range of modulation higher or lower.

Summing and Mixing

CV signals can be combined using CV mixers. Summing two distinct voltages creates complex, interdependent modulation shapes. For instance, mixing a slow LFO with a fast random voltage yields a modulation source that drifts broadly while exhibiting localized jitter, introducing organic instability to any connected audio parameter.

Slew Limiting

A slew limiter smooths out abrupt transitions between voltage levels. When applied to stepped pitch voltages from a sequencer, it creates portamento or glide effects by forcing the voltage to slide gradually from one note to the next.

Quantization

Analog CV from sequencers, touch plates, or random sources often produces continuous voltages that do not conform to traditional musical scales. Pitch quantizers read incoming continuous voltages and snap them to the nearest predetermined interval, such as chromatic semitones or specific diatonic scales, ensuring harmonic coherence.

Audio-Rate Modulation and Synthesis Application

When CV signals are driven into audio-rate frequencies (above 20 Hz), the distinction between control signals and audible sound blurs. Running an audio oscillator directly into the CV input of another oscillator produces Frequency Modulation (FM). Routing audio-rate CV into a Voltage-Controlled Filter (VCF) generates rich sidebands and complex overtone structures.

Ultimately, modular synthesizers achieve complex sound design by processing these control voltages as independent, malleable electrical signals, turning the physical manipulation of electricity into acoustic expression.
