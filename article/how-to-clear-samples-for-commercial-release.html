How to Clear Samples for Commercial Release

Clearing a sample requires securing legal permission from the owners of both the original sound recording and the underlying musical composition before a song is commercially distributed. Because copyright law treats the recorded audio and the written song as separate entities, producers must identify all rights holders, submit formal clearance requests, negotiate upfront advances and ongoing royalties, and execute binding contracts. Failing to secure these permissions leaves creators vulnerable to copyright infringement lawsuits, statutory damages, and the forced removal of their music from streaming platforms.

Step 1: Identify the Two Distinct Copyrights

Every commercial sound recording contains two separate layers of copyright protection that must be cleared independently:

  • The Master Recording: The specific audio recording you are sampling, typically owned or controlled by a record label or the independent artist who funded the session.
  • The Musical Composition: The underlying notes, melodies, lyrics, and arrangement, typically owned or administered by music publishers, performing rights organizations (PROs), or the original songwriters.

Step 2: Locate the Rights Holders

Before submitting a request, track down the exact parties who control both rights:

  • Finding Master Owners: Review album liner notes, metadata on streaming platforms, or databases like Discogs. For major releases, the label that released the track generally controls the master.
  • Finding Composition Owners: Search the public repertoire databases of PROs such as ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, or international counterparts like PRS and SACEM. These databases list the credited songwriters and their corresponding publishing administrators.

Step 3: Prepare and Submit a Clearance Request

Contact the licensing departments of the record label and the publisher(s) with a formal sample clearance package. To expedite the review process, provide:

  • The title, artist, and release date of the original track.
  • The exact timestamps and duration of the sample used (e.g., "0:12 to 0:16, looped throughout the chorus").
  • A copy of the original song and an unmastered MP3 of your new track with the sample clearly highlighted.
  • A description of the intended release, including distribution channels (streaming, physical, sync), release dates, and projected unit volumes.

Step 4: Negotiate Deal Terms

Both the master owner and the publishing owner will evaluate your request and set their terms. Common deal points include:

  • Master Use Fee: A one-time, upfront buyout fee or an advance against future royalties paid to the record label.
  • Master Royalty: A percentage of the artist's wholesale or streaming revenue from the new recording.
  • Publishing/Copyright Share: Publishers rarely accept flat fees alone; they typically demand a percentage of the new song's copyright ownership (ranging from 10% to over 50%, depending on the sample's prominence), entitling them to songwriting royalties.

Step 5: Execute Written Agreements

Clearance is not legally complete until formal contracts are signed by all authorized parties. Obtain two executed agreements:

  1. A Master Use License: Granted by the record label, authorizing the reproduction and distribution of the recorded audio snippet.
  2. A Mechanical/Sample Clearance Agreement: Granted by the publisher(s), licensing the underlying musical work and specifying the agreed-upon publishing splits and songwriter credits.

Alternatives to Traditional Clearance

If clearance is denied, too costly, or the original owners cannot be reached, alternative legal routes include:

  • Interpolation (Re-recording): Re-playing the melody or re-singing the lyrics in the studio eliminates the need for a master license, requiring only a composition clearance from the publisher.
  • Royalty-Free Sample Libraries: Utilizing pre-cleared audio packs from reputable vendors that grant commercial exploitation rights under standard end-user license agreements.
