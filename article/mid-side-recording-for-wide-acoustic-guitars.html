Mid-Side Recording for Wide Acoustic Guitars

Mid-side (M/S) recording is a powerful stereo technique that allows music producers to capture an expansive acoustic guitar soundstage while maintaining complete mono compatibility. By pairing a directional microphone facing the instrument with a bidirectional microphone capturing ambient side reflections, engineers can decode the signals into a dynamic stereo field. This article explains the necessary microphone setup, the DAW matrixing process required to decode the audio, and how to manipulate stereo width during mixing without introducing phase cancellation.

1. The Microphone Hardware Setup

The mid-side technique relies on two distinct microphones positioned with their capsules aligned as closely as possible to prevent phase arrival discrepancies.

  • The Mid Microphone: Typically a cardioid or omnidirectional condenser, pointed directly at the sweet spot of the acoustic guitar (usually between the 12th fret and the body). This captures the direct, centered sound of the performance.
  • The Side Microphone: A bidirectional (figure-8) microphone placed directly above or below the mid capsule. It is oriented perpendicular to the mid mic so that its two active lobes point to the left and right, capturing ambient room sound and off-axis instrument resonance while rejecting the direct sound coming from the front.

2. The DAW Routing and Decoding Process

Raw mid-side audio cannot be used directly on standard left-right stereo playback systems without decoding. While dedicated M/S decoder plugins exist, the traditional matrix routing in a digital audio workstation (DAW) involves three channels:

  1. Mid Channel: The signal from the mid microphone, kept in the center of the mix (panned dead center).
  2. Side Left Channel: The signal from the figure-8 microphone, panned 100% to the left.
  3. Side Right Channel: A duplicate of the figure-8 microphone signal, panned 100% to the right with its polarity (phase) inverted by 180 degrees.

Once grouped together, the two side channels form a cohesive stereo pair where identical off-axis signals push to the far extremes of the stereo field.

3. Controlling Stereo Width

Producers adjust the width of the acoustic guitar simply by changing the balance between the mid track and the side tracks:

  • Increasing Width: Raising the faders of the linked side channels relative to the mid channel increases room ambience and pushes the acoustic guitar wide across the stereo spectrum.
  • Focusing the Center: Lowering the side channels relative to the mid channel narrows the image, creating a focused, intimate acoustic guitar that leaves space in the stereo field for wide panning of other instruments, such as doubled electric guitars or backing vocals.

4. Flawless Mono Compatibility

The primary advantage of mid-side recording over other stereo methods—such as spaced pairs (A/B) or XY configurations—is absolute mono compatibility. When the track is summed to mono on small speakers, smartphones, or club systems, the side-left and side-right channels cancel each other out completely due to the inverted polarity. The result is the clean, uncolored mid signal without the hollow, comb-filtered artifacts common to other multi-mic stereo setups.
