Audio Buffer Size for Recording Live Instruments

When recording live instruments in music production, audio buffer size is one of the most critical settings in your Digital Audio Workstation (DAW). It directly dictates the balance between input latency—the delay between playing an instrument and hearing it in your headphones—and your computer’s processor performance. This article explains what buffer size is, why low buffer sizes are essential for tracking, the risks of setting it incorrectly, and the best practices for setting up your session to ensure a smooth, latency-free recording performance.

What Is Audio Buffer Size?

Audio buffer size refers to the amount of time a computer's CPU is given to process incoming and outgoing audio data before outputting it. It is measured in samples, typically in powers of two, such as 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1024 samples.

  • Small Buffer (e.g., 32 to 128 samples): The computer processes audio in very small chunks, resulting in extremely fast processing times and minimal delay (latency).
  • Large Buffer (e.g., 512 to 1024 samples): The computer has more time to process complex tasks, preventing system overloads at the expense of a noticeable delay.

The Impact on the Musician: Latency

When tracking a live instrument—such as an electric guitar, bass, acoustic instrument via a microphone, or MIDI keyboard—monitoring in real time is necessary.

If the buffer size is set too high (such as 512 or 1024 samples), the resulting round-trip latency often exceeds 10 to 20 milliseconds. This delay acts like a subtle slapback echo. For a musician, hearing a note even a fraction of a second after plucking a string or hitting a drum destroys rhythmic pocket, compromises groove, and makes tight performances nearly impossible.

To achieve a natural playing experience, total round-trip latency should remain under 5 to 7 milliseconds, which generally requires a buffer setting of 64 or 128 samples at standard sample rates (44.1 kHz or 48 kHz).

The CPU Trade-off: Clicks, Pops, and Dropouts

While a low buffer size is ideal for the performer, it demands significant processing power from the CPU. Because the computer must process incoming audio almost instantaneously, any temporary spike in system load can cause the processor to fall behind.

When the CPU fails to deliver the audio within the designated buffer window, the audio stream drops out momentarily. This manifests as audible clicks, pops, digital distortion, or session crashes.

Recommended Buffer Settings by Stage

  • Tracking/Recording (32–128 samples): Essential for software monitoring, virtual instrument playback, and tracking through software amp simulators or vocal effects.
  • Overdubbing in Moderate Projects (128–256 samples): A middle ground if your computer begins struggling with plugins while additional tracks are being recorded.
  • Mixing and Mastering (512–1024 samples): High latency is irrelevant because no live input is being monitored. The larger buffer allows the CPU to comfortably run dozens of processing-heavy plugins, reverbs, and virtual instruments without audio dropouts.

Best Practices for Live Tracking

  1. Track Early in the Production Process: Record your live instruments before loading the project with CPU-intensive mixing plugins.
  2. Freeze or Disable Heavy Tracks: If you must track late in a project, freeze or bounce virtual instruments and disable resource-heavy plugins to free up CPU headroom for a low buffer setting.
  3. Use Direct Monitoring: Many modern audio interfaces offer zero-latency direct hardware monitoring. This routes the input signal straight to the headphones before it reaches the computer, completely bypassing DAW buffer latency. This allows you to track comfortably even if your session requires a higher buffer size.
  4. Enable Low Latency Monitoring Modes: Most major DAWs feature a dedicated "Low Latency Mode" that temporarily bypasses latency-inducing plugins during tracking to keep monitoring responsive.
