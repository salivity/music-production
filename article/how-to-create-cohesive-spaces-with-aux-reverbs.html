How to Create Cohesive Spaces with Aux Reverbs

Using shared auxiliary (aux) reverbs is a foundational mixing technique that allows producers to place distinct instruments into a single, convincing acoustic environment. By sending multiple audio tracks to a centralized reverb return rather than placing individual reverb plugins on every channel, producers establish sonic cohesion, maintain clarity in the mix, and optimize CPU performance. This guide explains how to set up shared aux reverbs, control virtual depth, and process the return channels to produce clean, professional ambient spaces.

The Mechanism of Shared Aux Reverbs

In a digital audio workstation (DAW), an aux reverb works via a send-and-return system. An auxiliary track is created with a reverb plugin set to 100% wet—meaning only the reverberated signal is generated. Individual instrument tracks then use channel sends to feed a duplicate of their signal to this aux channel.

Using individual insert reverbs on every track introduces multiple conflicting reflections, room shapes, and decay times. This clutters the frequency spectrum and makes instruments sound as though they were recorded in completely different buildings. A shared aux reverb acts as a virtual room that binds the disparate sound sources together, creating the illusion of a shared physical performance space.

Controlling Front-to-Back Depth

A shared reverb allows producers to position elements along the depth axis of a mix. In human hearing, sounds with a higher ratio of direct-to-reverberant sound appear closer, while sounds with more reflected energy appear farther away.

Producers manipulate this depth perception purely through send levels:

  • Foreground elements: Lead vocals, kick drums, and bass lines typically receive very little or no send to the shared reverb, keeping them upfront, dry, and impactful.
  • Midground elements: Rhythm guitars, snare drums, and synthesizers receive a moderate send level, placing them just behind the lead elements.
  • Background elements: Backing vocals, secondary percussion, pads, and ambient textures receive higher send amounts, pushing them deeper into the virtual room to create dimension.

Because every element shares the exact same reflection patterns and decay characteristics, the depth sounds natural rather than disjointed.

Structuring a Multi-Reverb Space

While a single shared reverb provides maximum cohesion, producers often use a template of two or three shared spaces to handle different spatial duties without creating clutter:

  1. The Short Room (Ambience): A small room or studio space with a decay time under one second. This is used on drums and percussion to give them a natural acoustic imprint and body without adding a noticeable tail.
  2. The Medium Plate or Chamber: A classic plate setting with a decay between 1.2 and 2.5 seconds. This provides smooth decay and sheen for lead vocals, electric guitars, and solo instruments.
  3. The Long Hall or Space: A larger acoustic environment with a decay exceeding three seconds, used sparingly on pads, transitional effects, and atmospheric elements to add epic scale.

By restricting the entire production to these standardized spaces, the mix retains variety while remaining structurally cohesive.

Sculpting the Reverb Return

To prevent a shared reverb from muddying the overall mix, producers treat the aux return channel as an independent instrument that requires processing:

  • The Abbey Road EQ Filter: Filtering the input or output of the reverb channel by high-passing up to 500–600 Hz and low-passing down to 7–10 kHz. Cutting the low end prevents muddy bass buildup, while rolling off the extreme highs removes harsh sibilance and digital splash.
  • Pre-Delay Adjustments: Adding 20 to 60 milliseconds of pre-delay separates the dry signal from the start of the reverb tail. This preserves the transient attack of vocals and drums, keeping them clear and present before the ambient space blooms.
  • Sidechain Ducking: Placing a compressor on the reverb aux track keyed to the lead vocal or snare. When the dry instrument plays, the reverb ducks down slightly, maintaining mix clarity. When the instrument stops, the reverb blooms upward to fill the void, creating seamless ambience without masking key elements.
