How Limiters Prevent Inter-Sample Peak Distortion

Digital limiters prevent inter-sample peak distortion during mastering by using oversampling and reconstruction algorithms to detect and manage analog waveform peaks that standard sample-peak meters miss. While standard digital meters only evaluate individual discrete samples, the continuous analog waveform reconstructed by a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) can rise above these sample points and exceed 0 dBFS, causing harsh clipping distortion. True Peak limiters solve this problem by upsampling the audio internally, identifying hidden peaks between samples, and applying gain reduction to keep the final reconstructed signal within safe analog playback limits.

Understanding Inter-Sample Peaks

Digital audio captures sound as discrete numerical snapshots taken at regular intervals, known as the sample rate. When audio is played back, a DAC smooths these discrete points back into a continuous analog voltage wave.

A traditional digital limiter monitors only the numerical value of each individual sample. If two consecutive samples sit at or near 0 dBFS, the continuous analog curve drawn between them often arcs significantly higher than the individual points. This hidden crest is an inter-sample peak (ISP). When an ISP exceeds the maximum ceiling of 0 dBFS, it overloads the DAC's analog reconstruction filters, generating audible clipping, harmonic distortion, and loss of transient clarity. ISPs also degrade lossy encoding processes, such as MP3 or AAC conversion on streaming platforms, generating encoding artifacts.

Internal Oversampling and Reconstruction

To catch inter-sample peaks before audio leaves the digital workstation, modern mastering limiters implement internal oversampling, often adhering to the ITU-R BS.1770 standard for True Peak measurement.

When audio enters a True Peak limiter, the processor temporarily multiplies the sample rate—typically by 4x, 8x, or 16x—using digital interpolation filters. By inserting intermediate calculated values between the original audio samples, the limiter reconstructs a high-resolution approximation of the continuous waveform. This process exposes the peaks that would otherwise only appear after digital-to-analog conversion.

Dynamic Gain Reduction and Lookahead Processing

Once oversampling reveals the true trajectory of the waveform, the limiter's gain control module acts upon these hidden peaks.

True Peak limiters rely heavily on lookahead buffers. Because inter-sample peaks often occur on extremely fast transients (such as snare hits or bright synth attacks), the limiter delays the output signal by a few milliseconds. This lookahead window gives the detection circuit time to anticipate an incoming peak and smoothly apply gain reduction before the peak reaches the output stage.

The limiter pulls down the signal according to user-defined attack and release curves. By attenuating the discrete samples prior to the peak, the resulting continuous waveform remains safely below the defined threshold, eliminating inter-sample overshoots. Once processing is complete, the audio is downsampled back to the project’s target sample rate, retaining the gain reduction needed to keep the reconstructed analog wave below clipping levels.

Practical Application in Mastering

Handling inter-sample peaks effectively requires setting appropriate True Peak ceilings rather than relying solely on the limiter's internal processing. Even with oversampling, steep low-pass filtering during downsampling or subsequent lossy encoding can introduce minor post-limiter peaks.

Mastering engineers typically configure the True Peak limiter ceiling between -0.5 dBTP and -1.0 dBTP. This provides sufficient headroom for lossy compression codecs and consumer-grade DAC reconstruction filters, ensuring that the mastered track translates cleanly across diverse playback systems without unexpected distortion.
