How to Prepare Vinyl-Ready Master Files

Preparing a master for vinyl requires adapting digital audio to the mechanical constraints of a physical playback medium. Unlike digital formats, vinyl records rely on a physical needle tracing a microgroove, meaning extreme frequencies, wide stereo imaging, and aggressive limiting can cause physical playback distortion or needle skipping. This guide outlines the core technical considerations required to create clean, distortion-free, and dynamic vinyl-ready masters.

Low-End Management and Phase Correlation

Low frequencies demand the largest physical excursions from the cutting stylus. If deep bass contains stereo information or is out of phase between the left and right channels, it forces the cutting stylus to move vertically rather than horizontally. Excessive vertical movement can result in a groove that is too shallow, causing the playback stylus to jump out of the track.

  • Mono the Low Frequencies: Sum all frequencies below 100 Hz to 150 Hz to mono using an elliptical equalizer (EE).
  • Control Extreme Sub-Bass: Apply a high-pass filter between 20 Hz and 30 Hz (with a gentle 12 dB or 18 dB per octave slope) to remove non-audible sub-bass that wastes groove space and overworks the cutting head.

High-Frequency Control and De-Essing

The playback stylus struggles to trace rapid, sharp physical modulations at high frequencies. Harsh transients, aggressive cymbal crashes, and vocal sibilance can cause severe tracking distortion or overheat the lathe’s cutting head.

  • Tame Sibilance: Apply precise de-essing to vocals, targeting harsh spikes between 5 kHz and 10 kHz.
  • Gentle High-End Roll-off: Avoid the bright, ultra-extended top end typical of digital masters. Use high-shelf attenuation or dynamic EQ to smooth out frequencies above 15 kHz.

Dynamic Range Preservation

Vinyl inherently possesses a lower signal-to-noise ratio than 24-bit digital audio, but it performs poorly when subjected to heavy brickwall limiting. Heavily squashed audio with a low crest factor translates to a quiet, flat-sounding record because the lathe operator must turn down the overall cutting level to prevent distortion.

  • Avoid Brickwall Limiting: Deliver files with transient peaks intact. Let the cutting engineer use their specialized analog limiters.
  • Provide Headroom: Keep peak levels around -1.0 dBFS to -0.5 dBFS to prevent inter-sample peaks from clipping digital-to-analog converters during the cutting stage.

Track Sequencing and Inner Groove Distortion

As the stylus travels inward toward the center label, the linear velocity of the vinyl decreases. This reduction in surface speed means that high frequencies must be packed into a much smaller physical area, making tracking difficult for playback cartridges.

  • Sequencing Strategy: Place louder, brighter, and bass-heavy tracks near the outer edge (the beginning of Side A and Side B).
  • Inner Tracks: Place quieter tracks, ballads, or acoustic arrangements closer to the center of the record to minimize inner groove distortion.

Side Lengths and Cutting Levels

The length of music on a record directly dictates how wide the cutting engineer can cut the grooves. Longer running times force narrower, shallower grooves, which lowers the overall playback volume and raises the surface-noise floor.

  • 12-inch at 33 ⅓ RPM: Optimal quality is achieved at 18 to 22 minutes per side. Exceeding 22 minutes significantly degrades volume and bass response.
  • 12-inch at 45 RPM: Optimal quality is between 12 and 15 minutes per side.

Delivery Specifications

  • Resolution: Export files at their native sample rate (e.g., 48 kHz, 88.2 kHz, or 96 kHz) at 24-bit or 32-bit float without downsampling or applying dither.
  • File Structure: Deliver masters either as two continuous audio files (one for Side A, one for Side B) with exact spacing included, or as individual tracks paired with a comprehensive PQ sheet and cue sheet detailing exact track times, gaps, and side splits.
