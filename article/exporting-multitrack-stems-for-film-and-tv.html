Exporting Multitrack Stems for Film and TV

Exporting multitrack stems for film and television requires strict adherence to industry-standard technical specifications to ensure seamless integration into the final re-recording mix. This guide outlines the essential technical requirements for delivering music stems, including standard sample rates, bit depths, sync alignment, headroom, stem configurations, and file naming conventions.

Audio Format and Resolution

The film and broadcast industries rely on standardized digital audio formats. Deviating from these settings can cause sample-rate conversion errors or pitch artifacts during the dub stage.

  • File Format: Broadcast Wave Format (BWF / .wav) is the universal industry standard. BWF files support embedded timecode metadata.
  • Sample Rate: 48 kHz is the mandatory standard for film and television. In some high-end film pipelines, 96 kHz may be requested, but you should never deliver 44.1 kHz.
  • Bit Depth: 24-bit fixed or 32-bit floating point. 24-bit is the most widely compatible, while 32-bit float provides extra protection against digital clipping.

Timecode and Synchronization

All stems must line up instantly without manual positioning by the re-recording mixer.

  • Common Start Point: Every exported stem must share the exact same start and end points on the timeline, starting from bar 1, beat 1 (or the designated cue start point), even if a specific instrument does not play until midway through the cue.
  • Sync Pop (2-Pop): Include a standard 1-frame, 1 kHz visual/audio sync tone located exactly two seconds before the first frame of picture (typically at 00:59:58:00 for an hour-one start).
  • Frame Rate: Ensure your digital audio workstation (DAW) project matches the exact frame rate of the video cut (e.g., 23.976 fps, 24 fps, 25 fps, or 29.97 fps).

Headroom and Master Bus Processing

Music stems will be balanced against dialogue, sound effects, and Foley. Re-recording mixers require dynamic headroom to adjust music levels appropriately.

  • Remove Master Limiters: Turn off aggressive brickwall limiters, clippers, or heavy mastering compression on the master output before printing stems.
  • Maintain Headroom: Ensure stems peak between -3 dBFS and -6 dBFS. No individual stem should hit 0 dBFS.
  • Sum to Mix: When all exported stems are brought into a blank session with faders set to unity (0 dB), the combined playback must sound identical to your approved full stereo mix.

Stem Categorization and FX Delivery

A stem is not an individual solo track (which is a multitrack or "part"); it is a stereo audio group representing a specific instrument family.

Standard delivery typically requires the following stereo groups:

  • Strings (High Strings, Low Strings, or unified)
  • Brass
  • Woodwinds
  • Percussion / Drums (often split into Tuned/Orchestral and Modern/Acoustic Kit)
  • Bass (Synth Bass, Electric Bass, or Upright)
  • Guitars / Keys
  • Synths / Sound Design
  • Choir / Vocals

Effects Routing: Reverb and time-based effects should generally be printed within their corresponding stems. If a reverb is shared across multiple instrument families (such as an orchestral hall reverb), print a dedicated "Shared FX" or "Reverb" stem to prevent the master balance from altering when individual sections are adjusted.

File Naming and Delivery Package

Unambiguous file naming prevents critical mix-stage delays.

Use a standardized naming convention: [Cue Number]_[Cue Title]_[Stem Name]_[Version].wav

Example:

  • 1M03_ChasingShadows_STRINGS_v02.wav
  • 1M03_ChasingShadows_PERC_v02.wav
  • 1M03_ChasingShadows_FULLMIX_v02.wav

Always include a full stereo reference mix of the cue, an unmastered mix, and a tempo map or MIDI file if requested by the music editor.
