Recording Acoustic Piano with Stereo Pairs

Capturing an acoustic piano in music production requires balancing its massive frequency range, dynamic sensitivity, and physical size. Using a stereo pair of microphones is the industry standard approach to achieve a realistic width, natural spatial depth, and proper tonal balance. This guide outlines the most common stereo miking configurations—including Spaced Pair (A/B), XY Coincident, ORTF, and Blumlein—explaining how each functions, their tonal characteristics, and how to place them for optimal results.

1. Spaced Pair (A/B)

The Spaced Pair technique utilizes two identical microphones—typically small or large-diaphragm cardioid or omnidirectional condensers—placed a set distance apart.

  • Placement: Position one microphone over the lower strings (toward the bass end) and the second over the higher strings (toward the treble end), roughly 12 to 24 inches above the strings.
  • Sound Profile: Produces a wide, dramatic stereo spread with clear separation between the left and right hands of the player.
  • Phase Considerations: Because the microphones receive sound waves at different times, phase cancellation can occur. To mitigate phase issues, adhere to the 3:1 rule: the distance between the two microphones should be at least three times the distance from each microphone to the sound source.

2. XY Coincident Pair

The XY configuration uses two directional microphones (typically cardioids) mounted with their capsules aligned vertically as close as possible without touching, typically angled between 90° and 135°.

  • Placement: Often positioned just outside or directly over the piano rim, aimed toward the center of the harp where the hammers strike the strings.
  • Sound Profile: Delivers a focused, cohesive sound with an accurate center image. The stereo spread is narrower compared to spaced techniques, but the image is very stable.
  • Phase Considerations: Because the capsules are placed at virtually the same point in space, sound arrives at both capsules simultaneously. This provides near-perfect mono compatibility with zero phase cancellation.

3. ORTF (Near-Coincident Pair)

Developed by the French Broadcasting Organization (Office de Radiodiffusion Télévision Française), ORTF mimics the spacing and angling of human ears.

  • Placement: Two cardioid microphones are set with their capsules 17 centimeters (about 6.7 inches) apart at an angle of 110°. Place the array roughly 1 to 3 feet back from the piano case or directly over the soundboard.
  • Sound Profile: Offers a balanced compromise between the broad stereo width of a spaced pair and the precise imaging of an XY setup. It provides natural ambient depth while preserving clarity.
  • Phase Considerations: Mono compatibility is good, though not as absolute as coincident techniques.

4. Blumlein Pair

The Blumlein technique utilizes two bidirectional (figure-8) microphones positioned with their capsules aligned at a 90° angle to one another.

  • Placement: Typically positioned outside the piano near the curved edge, or just inside the opened lid pointed toward the soundboard.
  • Sound Profile: Creates an exceptionally realistic three-dimensional stereo image. The front lobes capture direct sound from the piano, while the rear lobes capture the natural reverberation of the recording space.
  • Phase Considerations: Coincident alignment prevents phase smear, but because the rear lobes pick up room reflections, this technique requires an acoustically treated or pleasant-sounding room.

Microphone Positioning Considerations

Regardless of the stereo array chosen, placement relative to the instrument drastically alters the tonal response:

  • Over the Hammers: Emphasizes percussive attack, transient brightness, and key action. Ideal for dense pop, rock, and modern productions where the piano needs to cut through a mix.
  • Over the Soundboard: Captures warmth, sustain, and fundamental body with less mechanical noise. Suited for ballads, ambient tracks, and cinematic scores.
  • Outside the Lid (Player Perspective): Positioning the stereo pair just behind the pianist's head or slightly outside the lid captures the instrument as an integrated whole, blending the direct strings with the natural acoustics of the room. This is standard for jazz and classical recordings.
