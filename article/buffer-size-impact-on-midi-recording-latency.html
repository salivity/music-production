Buffer Size Impact on MIDI Recording Latency

Audio buffer size directly dictates the delay between triggering a MIDI controller and hearing the generated sound from a virtual instrument. Lowering the buffer size minimizes this latency to produce a responsive, real-time playing experience, while increasing it relieves processor strain at the expense of noticeable delay. Understanding this trade-off is essential for capturing accurate, natural musical performances without experiencing system instability or audio dropouts.

The Role of Audio Buffers in MIDI Tracking

When you play a MIDI keyboard, the controller transmits digital performance data—such as pitch, velocity, and timing—almost instantly. However, that data must be translated into audible sound by a software instrument (VST/AU) inside your Digital Audio Workstation (DAW).

The audio buffer is a temporary holding area where your computer processes these digital audio calculations before sending them to your audio interface. Because sound generation relies on your audio engine rather than just raw MIDI data, the audio buffer determines how quickly you hear the synthesized sound.

Low Buffer Sizes: Minimizing Latency for Performance

A low buffer size (typically 32, 64, or 128 samples) instructs the computer to process audio in very small chunks.

  • The Advantage: Latency is reduced to an imperceptible range, generally between 1 and 5 milliseconds. This immediate auditory feedback is vital for expressive playing, tight rhythmic timing, and natural performance dynamics.
  • The Trade-Off: The CPU must empty and refill the buffer at an extremely rapid pace. If the processor cannot keep up—especially when using resource-intensive virtual instruments or effect plugins—the audio stream will suffer from clicks, pops, or total dropouts known as buffer underruns.

High Buffer Sizes: Stability at the Cost of Timing

A high buffer size (typically 512, 1024, or 2048 samples) gives the CPU more time to process complex audio signals before sending them to the outputs.

  • The Advantage: System stability increases significantly. The CPU can comfortably handle dense arrangements, heavy polyphony, and complex processing chains without glitching.
  • The Trade-Off: Round-trip latency increases to 10–30 milliseconds or higher. At these levels, musicians experience a distinct, unnatural delay between pressing a key and hearing the note. This latency disrupts muscle memory, often causing the performer to rush or play off-beat to compensate for the delayed sound.

Recommended Workflow for Music Production

To maintain optimal performance throughout the production process, standard practice involves alternating buffer settings based on the task:

  1. Recording Phase: Set the buffer size to 64 or 128 samples. Freeze unnecessary tracks and temporarily bypass heavy mastering or mixing plugins to keep CPU usage low.
  2. Mixing and Editing Phase: Once MIDI parts are tracked and quantized, raise the buffer size to 512 or 1024 samples. This frees up computing power to run advanced reverbs, compressors, and extensive plugin chains smoothly.
