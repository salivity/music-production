Optical vs FET Compressor Response Times for Vocals

Choosing between an optical and a FET compressor for vocal mixing comes down to how their distinct response times shape dynamics, transients, and tone. Optical compressors rely on light-dependent circuitry that creates slow, smooth, and program-dependent attack and release behaviors, making them ideal for transparent leveling. In contrast, Field Effect Transistor (FET) compressors operate at lightning-fast speeds measured in microseconds, excelling at aggressive transient control and peak limiting. This guide breaks down the technical differences in their response times and explains how to apply each effectively on vocal tracks.

Understanding Optical Compressor Response Times

Optical compressors (such as the classic Teletronix LA-2A) use an internal light source—typically an electroluminescent panel or LED—and a photosensitive cell (photocell) to detect audio levels and trigger gain reduction.

  • Attack Time: Optical units generally feature fixed, relatively slow attack times hovering around 10 milliseconds. Because the photocell requires time to react to the light emitter, rapid initial spikes slip through unaltered.
  • Release Time: The release curve of an optical compressor is unique because it is "program-dependent." The photocell has an inherent physical memory; release occurs in stages. Typically, the first 50% of gain release happens quickly (around 60 milliseconds), while the remaining attenuation releases gradually over one to several seconds.
  • Effect on Vocals: This sluggish, two-stage recovery curve yields a remarkably musical and transparent performance. Because the compressor does not clamp down instantly on plosives or sharp consonants, the vocal retains its natural dynamic openness while large dynamic swings across verses and choruses are kept consistently balanced.

Understanding FET Compressor Response Times

FET compressors (epitomized by the UREi 1176) replace traditional optical elements or variable-mu tubes with solid-state field-effect transistors to govern attenuation.

  • Attack Time: FET compressors are legendary for extreme speed. Their attack times range from roughly 20 to 800 microseconds (millionths of a second). Even at its slowest setting, an 1176 is significantly faster than most optical compressors at their fastest.
  • Release Time: Release settings on FET units typically range from 50 milliseconds to 1.1 seconds. This allows the compressor to snap back into place almost instantly after a loud burst of sound.
  • Effect on Vocals: The ultra-fast attack enables FET compressors to instantly clamp down on hard consonants, sudden vocal spikes, and aggressive shouts. The swift release can create perceived loudness and punch by pulling up low-level vocal details and breath sounds immediately after a transient passes. When pushed hard, this rapid cycling also introduces harmonic saturation, helping a vocal cut through a dense modern mix.

Key Functional Differences on Vocals

  1. Transient Handling: FET compressors grab and reshape the leading edge of a vocal word or syllable immediately, preventing digital clipping and pulling back sudden leaps in dynamic range. Optical compressors let those initial transients breathe, smoothing out the body of the phrase rather than shaving off the front edge.
  2. Predictability vs. Adaptation: A FET compressor follows its calibrated settings precisely, offering rigid, mechanical consistency from one peak to the next. An optical compressor reacts to the overall density of the vocal performance—compressing heavier and releasing slower during sustained, loud passages, and recovering faster during sparse phrases.
  3. Pumping and Artifacts: Mismanaging an ultra-fast FET release can cause audible pumping or gritty distortion on sustained low-frequency vowel sounds. Optical compressors are virtually immune to aggressive pumping due to their soft release curves, offering natural leveling even under heavy gain reduction.

Combining Both: Serial Compression

In professional vocal production, mixing engineers rarely rely solely on one response profile. A common practice is serial compression, which leverages the strengths of both circuits:

  1. The FET Compressor First: Insert a FET compressor with a fast attack and moderate release to shave off only the highest 2 dB to 4 dB of erratic peaks and consonants.
  2. The Optical Compressor Second: Follow with an optical compressor applying an additional 2 dB to 3 dB of gentle, average gain reduction.

By allowing the fast FET response to absorb the shock of transient spikes, the optical compressor can perform its smooth, musical leveling without being tripped up by sudden consonants, delivering a vocal that sounds both polished and dynamic.
