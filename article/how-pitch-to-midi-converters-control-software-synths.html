How Pitch to MIDI Converters Control Software Synths

Pitch-to-MIDI converters bridge the gap between traditional acoustic instruments and digital music production by translating raw acoustic audio into standard MIDI data in real time. This process allows instruments such as guitars, brass, woodwinds, strings, and even the human voice to trigger and manipulate virtual software synthesizers within a digital audio workstation (DAW). By analyzing incoming audio frequencies, dynamics, and articulation, these converters map acoustic performance nuances directly to digital sound engines.

1. Audio Capture and Pre-Processing

The process begins by capturing the acoustic instrument's output using standard microphones or specialized contact pickups (such as hexaphonic pickups for guitars). The converter cleans up the incoming analog audio signal through filtering, removing unwanted low-end rumble or excessive high-frequency noise that could interfere with frequency analysis.

2. Pitch Detection and Frequency Analysis

Once the audio signal is clean, the converter analyzes its fundamental frequency (\(f_0\)). Pitch-tracking algorithms—such as autocorrelation, zero-crossing detection, or Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)—measure the waveform cycles to identify the exact note being played.

  • Monophonic Tracking: Standard converters track single-note lines, which works well for monophonic acoustic instruments like trumpets, saxophones, flutes, or solo vocal lines.
  • Polyphonic Tracking: Advanced software and multi-pickup systems can analyze complex, overlapping harmonic content, deciphering chords and multiple simultaneous notes on instruments like the acoustic guitar or piano.

3. Translation into MIDI Data

After identifying the acoustic properties of the incoming audio, the converter generates standard MIDI messages:

  • Note-On and Note-Off: When a note starts, the detected frequency is matched to the nearest MIDI note number (e.g., 440 Hz corresponds to A4, MIDI note 69). When the audio signal drops below a designated threshold, a Note-Off message is sent.
  • Velocity: The converter reads the amplitude and attack transient of the note strikes to determine velocity values (0–127), reflecting how softly or aggressively the acoustic instrument was played.
  • Pitch Bend: Subtle pitch variations, vibrato, or slides that fall between standard semitones are converted into continuous MIDI Pitch Bend data, preserving the organic expression of the physical performance.
  • Continuous Controllers (CC): Sustained volume changes or dynamic swells can be mapped to MIDI CC11 (Expression) or CC7 (Volume).

4. Routing to Software Synthesizers

The generated MIDI messages are transmitted via a virtual MIDI bus, USB, or hardware MIDI interface directly into a DAW. Inside the DAW, this data is routed to a software synthesizer track. The synth interprets these messages identically to commands received from a traditional MIDI keyboard, generating digital waveforms, wavetable textures, or sampled sounds in direct response to the acoustic instrument.

5. Practical Considerations: Latency and Tracking

Translating audio to MIDI inherently requires a fraction of a waveform cycle to detect pitch, meaning lower notes (which have longer wavelengths) require slightly more processing time than higher notes. To maintain clean tracking and minimize latency, performers typically optimize input gain, adjust sensitivity thresholds, and play with clean articulation to prevent accidental trigger artifacts or double notes.
