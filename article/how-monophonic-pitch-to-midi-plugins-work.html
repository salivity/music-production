How Monophonic Pitch to MIDI Plugins Work

Converting monophonic audio into MIDI is a foundational process in modern music production that allows producers to control virtual instruments using their voice or an acoustic instrument. This article explains the technical pipeline pitch-tracking plugins use to transform continuous audio waveforms into discrete digital performance data. By breaking down signal pre-processing, fundamental frequency estimation, amplitude tracking, and final MIDI message generation, you will gain a clear understanding of the digital signal processing (DSP) driving these tools.

1. Signal Pre-Processing

Before a plugin can evaluate pitch, the incoming audio must be conditioned to remove acoustic artifacts that could confuse the detection algorithm. Monophonic pitch detection requires a clear, single-voice source.

During pre-processing, the plugin applies:

  • High-Pass and Low-Pass Filtering: Removes sub-bass rumble, microphone handling noise, and high-frequency hiss that could skew frequency calculations.
  • Transient Detection: Identifies the precise attack of a sound to mark where a note begins, separating the initial strike from the sustained tone.

2. Pitch Detection Algorithms

The core task of the plugin is determining the fundamental frequency (\(f_0\)) of the sound, which determines the perceived musical pitch. Because complex acoustic sounds contain multiple overtones and harmonics, the plugin relies on specialized algorithms to isolate the root pitch:

  • Autocorrelation (Time-Domain): This method compares the incoming waveform against time-delayed versions of itself. When the delayed signal closely matches the original signal, the time offset reveals the fundamental period of the waveform. The frequency is then calculated as the inverse of this period (\(f = 1 / T\)).
  • Fast Fourier Transform / Spectral Analysis (Frequency-Domain): The plugin converts audio slices from the time domain into the frequency spectrum. It identifies the lowest strong harmonic peak or looks for common harmonic spacing to deduce the fundamental pitch.
  • The YIN Algorithm: A refined version of autocorrelation widely used in audio plugins. It minimizes errors caused by higher harmonics masquerading as the fundamental pitch (an issue known as octave jumping).

3. Velocity and Dynamics Mapping

Pitch tracking plugins capture not only which note was played, but how hard it was played.

To determine the MIDI velocity (a value ranging from 0 to 127), an envelope follower tracks the amplitude of the incoming signal. The peak amplitude or Root Mean Square (RMS) energy measured immediately after the note's transient attack is scaled and mapped directly to a corresponding MIDI velocity value.

4. Note Segmentation and Thresholds

To prevent erratic, unintentional MIDI notes, the plugin employs strict thresholds:

  • Gate / Amplitude Threshold: Sounds falling below a specified decibel level are ignored, preventing background noise from triggering notes.
  • Hysteresis: Plugins use dual-threshold systems to differentiate between intentional note releases and temporary amplitude dips.
  • Pitch Stability Checks: The algorithm requires the frequency to remain stable for a minimum number of milliseconds before generating a Note On message. When the signal drops below the threshold or changes frequency abruptly, a Note Off message is sent.

5. Pitch Bend and Continuous Expression

Monophonic sources like vocals, fretless basses, or brass instruments rarely stay on an exact, mathematically perfect pitch. To reflect real-world performance nuances:

  • The plugin quantizes the fundamental frequency to the nearest standard MIDI note number (for example, 440 Hz maps to Note 69, or A4).
  • Any microtonal deviations, vibrato, or slides are calculated as deviations from the center frequency.
  • These micro-deviations are continuously output as high-resolution MIDI Pitch Bend messages, preserving the human inflection of the original performance.

Latency Considerations

Real-time monophonic pitch tracking inherently introduces slight latency. A pitch detector must analyze at least one to two full cycles of a waveform to reliably identify its period. Consequently, lower frequencies (which have longer physical cycles) require slightly more processing time to detect than higher frequencies. Modern plugins balance buffer sizes and predictive algorithms to keep this delay imperceptible during recording and playback.
