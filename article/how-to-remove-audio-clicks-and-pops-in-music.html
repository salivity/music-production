How to Remove Audio Clicks and Pops in Music

Unwanted clicks, pops, and digital artifacts can instantly ruin an otherwise exceptional music recording. This guide provides a direct overview of the primary causes of transient audio artifacts, the industry-standard software tools used to eliminate them, and the manual and automated repair techniques required to restore pristine audio fidelity without degrading the original performance.

Common Causes of Clicks and Pops

Clicks and pops typically stem from two distinct sources: acoustic anomalies and digital errors. Acoustic artifacts include mouth noises, vocal plosives, and physical contact with instruments or microphones. Digital artifacts usually result from buffer underruns during recording, mismatched sample rates, clock jitter between digital devices, or hard cuts in audio regions that lack crossfades. Identifying the source helps determine whether a manual or automated restoration approach is necessary.

Essential Software Tools

  • Dedicated Restoration Suites: Tools like iZotope RX (specifically the De-click and Mouth De-click modules), Celemony Capstan, and Acon Digital Acoustica are industry standards. They feature algorithms designed to isolate transient spikes from musical transients like drum hits.
  • Spectral Editors: Software such as Steinberg SpectraLayers or the spectral display inside your DAW allows you to visualize audio as frequencies over time, making it easy to spot isolated vertical spikes that represent clicks.
  • DAW Pencil Tools: Most major digital audio workstations (including Pro Tools, Logic Pro, and Studio One) feature a pencil or draw tool that lets you redraw the waveform directly at the sample level.

Primary Techniques for Removal

1. Manual Waveform Redrawing

For isolated, high-amplitude digital clicks that occur over just a few samples, manual editing provides the most transparent fix.

  • Zoom into the audio file down to the individual sample level.
  • Locate the sharp discontinuity or jagged spike in the waveform.
  • Use your DAW's pencil tool to smooth the curve across the gap, connecting the waveform smoothly to eliminate the sudden DC offset or digital drop.

2. Micro-Crossfades at Edit Points

Clicks frequently occur when audio clips are spliced together without smooth transitions.

  • Ensure all split points are placed as close to zero-crossings (where the waveform crosses the center amplitude line) as possible.
  • Apply short crossfades (typically 2 to 10 milliseconds) across every edit boundary. An equal-power crossfade prevents sudden drops in level while eliminating the transient click caused by abrupt waveform transitions.

3. Targeted Spectral Repair

When clicks overlap with complex musical material, such as an acoustic guitar strum or lead vocal, time-domain editing may create audible dropouts.

  • Open the audio track in a spectral editor.
  • Locate the click, which will appear as a narrow, bright vertical line extending across multiple frequency bands.
  • Select the artifact using a lasso or time-frequency selection tool.
  • Apply an "Interpolate" or "Replace" algorithm. The software analyzes the surrounding audio data and synthesizes a replacement for the selected area, completely removing the artifact without dampening the natural transient of the instrument.

4. Automated De-Clicking Plugins

For widespread issues like vinyl crackle, consistent vocal mouth clicks, or damaged multi-track recordings, automated restoration plugins save significant time.

  • Insert the de-clicker at the very beginning of the plugin chain, before any compression or saturation which can exaggerate artifacts.
  • Select the appropriate algorithm profile (e.g., "Mouth Click," "Digital Click," or "Low-End Thump").
  • Adjust the sensitivity control while monitoring only the removed signal (often labeled "Output Clicks Only" or "Difference"). Increase the sensitivity until the clicks are captured, but stop before you hear drum transients or vocal consonants bleeding into the removal buffer to prevent transient dulling.

Prevention Best Practices

While post-production restoration is powerful, preventing clicks during tracking is optimal. Always match hardware clocking over word clock or digital cables (ADAT/SPDIF), increase your audio interface buffer size when tracking large track counts, employ proper pop filters, and keep vocalists hydrated to minimize saliva-induced mouth noises.
