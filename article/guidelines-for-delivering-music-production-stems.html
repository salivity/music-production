Guidelines for Delivering Music Production Stems

Delivering finalized instrumental and vocal stems requires strict adherence to technical and organizational standards to ensure seamless integration into mastering, remixing, broadcasting, and sync licensing workflows. This guide outlines the essential industry requirements for commercial music production, covering the distinction between stems and multitracks, technical audio specifications, alignment protocols, dry versus wet options, and professional file-naming conventions.

Stems vs. Multitracks

In commercial audio production, a clear distinction exists between multitracks and stems:

  • Multitracks: Individual raw audio recordings (e.g., separate Kick In, Kick Out, Snare Top, Lead Vocal Mic).
  • Stems: Grouped stereo submixes of multiple tracks that make up the full mix (e.g., All Drums, All Bass, Guitars, Synths, Lead Vocals, Backing Vocals).

When a label, sync supervisor, or post-production house requests "stems," they generally expect consolidated stereo submixes that, when imported into a digital audio workstation (DAW) and set to unity gain (0 dB), reproduce the exact balance of the final mix.

Technical Audio Specifications

Deliver stem files according to the source session settings without upsampling or conversion:

  • File Format: Broadcast WAV (BWF) or standard 24-bit WAV/AIFF. Lossy formats such as MP3 or AAC are never acceptable for commercial deliverables.
  • Sample Rate: Match the native session rate—typically 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz for commercial music, or 48 kHz / 96 kHz for film and television sync.
  • Bit Depth: 24-bit or 32-bit floating point. 32-bit float prevents digital clipping if elements momentarily exceed 0 dBFS, though standard commercial practice expects signals to stay cleanly below full scale.
  • Headroom: Stems should not feature aggressive limiting on the master bus during export. Leave between -3 dB to -6 dB of headroom across individual stems to allow mastering engineers and re-recording mixers room for adjustment.

Timeline Alignment and the Null Test

Every stem must share an identical starting point (Bar 1, Beat 1 or timecode 00:00:00:00). Consolidate all tracks from the exact beginning of the session timeline so that the receiving engineer can drag and drop every file onto a timeline and have them play in perfect synchronization without manual editing.

Before delivering, perform a Summing / Null Check:

  1. Import all exported stems into a blank session.
  2. Set all faders to unity (0 dB).
  3. Play the stems alongside the approved final mix.
  4. Invert the polarity (phase) of the stems or the master mix.
  5. If the preparation is correct, the signals will cancel each other out completely (silence), confirming that no volume discrepancies, missing automation, or altered plugin parameters exist.

Wet vs. Dry Processing

Commercial deliverables typically require "wet" stems (containing the panning, volume automation, compression, EQ, and time-based effects like reverb and delay that define the mix). However, flexibility is critical:

  • Wet Stems: Standard requirement for sync and live performance playback.
  • Dry Stems: Often requested for remixers and Dolby Atmos spatial audio mixing engineers who need to place dry sources into a 3D soundfield.
  • Auxiliary FX Stems: If wet stems are delivered, it is best practice to deliver time-based effects (reverbs, delays, choruses) on their own dedicated stem tracks rather than baked into the direct instruments. This allows sync editors to cut vocal lines without cutting off reverb tails.

Standard Commercial Deliverable Structure

A complete commercial delivery package includes standard stem groups and utility mix passes:

1. Instrumental Stem Groups

  • Drums/Percussion: Combined drum kit, percussion, and electronic elements.
  • Bass: Combined synth bass, electric bass, and sub-bass.
  • Guitars: Electric and acoustic rhythm and lead guitars.
  • Keyboards/Synths: Pianos, organs, synthesizers, and pads.
  • Orchestral/Acoustic: Strings, brass, and woodwinds.
  • FX/Transitions: Risers, sweeps, and sound effects.

2. Vocal Stem Groups

  • Lead Vocals: Main vocal elements without harmonies or doubles.
  • Vocal FX: Dedicated delays and reverbs for the lead vocal.
  • Background Vocals: Harmonies, ad-libs, and doubles.

3. Full Mix Passes

  • Main Mix: Fully mastered and unmastered full mixes.
  • Instrumental Mix: The full mix with all vocal tracks muted.
  • TV/Performance Track: The full mix containing all instruments and backing vocals, with only the lead vocal muted.
  • Acapella: All vocal tracks playing together with no instrumentation.

File Naming Conventions and Delivery Packaging

Files should be instantly identifiable without opening them. Use a standardized, underscore-delimited format:

[SongTitle]_[Tempo-BPM]_[Key]_[StemName]_[WetOrDry]_[SampleRate-BitDepth]_[Version]

Example: SongName_120BPM_Amin_LeadVocals_Wet_48k24b_v1.wav

Deliver stems in an organized folder hierarchy (01_Master_Mixes, 02_Mix_Passes, 03_Stems_Wet, 04_Stems_Dry). Include a plain text file (README.txt) containing the track metadata: artist name, song title, tempo, key signature, sample rate, bit depth, timecode start points, and mixer contact information. Pack the files into an uncompressed ZIP or TAR archive, or transfer via enterprise platforms such as Aspera, Signiant, or secure cloud links.
