How to Remove Squeaks With Spectral Audio Repair

Acoustic recordings often capture mechanical artifacts such as finger squeaks on guitar strings and piano pedal thumps that can distract the listener. Spectral audio repair tools resolve this issue by converting standard audio waveforms into visual frequency maps, allowing audio engineers to surgically target and eliminate these noises without degrading the surrounding musical performance. This article explains how spectral processing works, the core mechanics behind identifying frequency anomalies, and the specific resynthesis techniques used to maintain natural acoustic tone.

Understanding the Spectrogram

Traditional audio editing relies on waveforms, which only display amplitude over time. Spectral editing relies on a visual representation called a spectrogram, generated through a mathematical process known as the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

A spectrogram maps three dimensions of audio simultaneously:

  • Time across the horizontal axis (X-axis).
  • Frequency up the vertical axis (Y-axis), typically ranging from 20 Hz to 20 kHz.
  • Amplitude represented by color intensity or brightness, where brighter areas indicate higher energy levels.

Because acoustic instruments have distinct harmonic profiles, musical notes appear as consistent, horizontal harmonic lines. Unwanted physical artifacts display entirely different visual signatures, making them easy to spot.

Identifying Acoustic Artifacts

Finger squeaks and mechanical pedal noises occupy distinct areas within the frequency spectrum:

  • Guitar String Squeaks: These are friction-based high-frequency bursts typically occurring between 2 kHz and 8 kHz. On a spectrogram, they appear as bright, vertical or diagonal slashes that cut across the sustained horizontal harmonics of the chord or note.
  • Piano Pedal Thumps and Mechanical Clunks: These are low-frequency, transient bursts usually centered between 30 Hz and 150 Hz. They present as dense, compact energy clusters at the bottom of the display, often accompanied by faint mechanical resonances in the lower-mid frequencies.

Surgical Isolation and Selection Tools

Unlike traditional equalizers that apply broad gain changes across an entire track, spectral editors allow the user to draw precise boundaries around only the noise itself. Engineers use specialized selection tools:

  • Lasso and Brush Tools: Enable freehand tracing around irregular high-frequency squeaks.
  • Time-Frequency Rectangles: Box in specific low-frequency thumps within fixed boundaries.
  • Harmonic Selection Tools: Automatically identify and exclude the actual musical harmonics from the selection, ensuring only the non-harmonic noise between the musical overtones is targeted.

Processing and Repair Techniques

Once an artifact is isolated, spectral software applies algorithmic processing to neutralize the noise. Three primary methods are used depending on the severity of the artifact:

1. Spectral Attenuation

The simplest approach is applying localized gain reduction specifically within the highlighted frequency window. Lowering the energy of a guitar squeak by 6 dB to 12 dB softens the harsh friction noise so it blends into the performance, rather than removing it entirely, which can sometimes sound unnatural.

2. Spectral Inpainting and Interpolation

When an artifact overlaps directly with a musical note, simple attenuation can leave an audible hole in the audio. Spectral inpainting analyzes the audio immediately preceding and following the selected area, as well as the audio frequencies directly above and below it. The algorithm then mathematically reconstructs (interpolates) the missing data, synthesizing replacement harmonics that seamlessly bridge the gap.

3. Frequency-Specific De-Clicking

For sharp mechanical transients, such as the initial click of a piano damper pedal engaging, algorithms detect the anomalous transient profile and separate it into tonal and noisy components. The tool dampens the transient burst while leaving the sustained resonance of the piano strings unaffected.

Preserving Natural Acoustic Dynamics

The primary advantage of spectral audio repair over traditional filtering, dynamic EQ, or noise gates is transparency. Traditional filters introduce phase distortion and dull the overall instrument tone across the duration of the track. Spectral repair operates non-destructively on isolated time-frequency cells, preserving the natural room reverberation, air, and organic decay essential to acoustic music.
