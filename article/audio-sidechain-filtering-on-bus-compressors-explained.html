Audio Sidechain Filtering on Bus Compressors Explained

Sidechain filtering on a bus compressor is a technique used during music production to control which frequencies trigger compression, preventing heavy low-end instruments from overpowering the entire mix. By applying a high-pass filter to the compressor's internal detection circuit, engineers prevent bass-heavy elements—such as kick drums and bass guitars—from causing excessive gain reduction and unnatural pumping. This article breaks down how internal sidechain filtering works, why it is essential for mix buses, and how to apply it effectively in your productions.

The Mechanism: Detection Circuit vs. Audio Path

To understand sidechain filtering, it is essential to distinguish between the two paths inside a compressor:

  1. The Audio Path: The actual sound signal passing through the processor to the output.
  2. The Detection (Sidechain) Circuit: The control mechanism that analyzes the incoming signal volume and tells the compressor when and how much to reduce the gain.

When you engage a sidechain filter, you are not filtering the audible audio signal. Instead, you are applying an equalizer—most commonly a high-pass filter (HPF)—exclusively to the detection circuit. The compressor simply "ignores" the filtered frequencies when calculating gain reduction, while the full frequency spectrum of your music continues to pass through unaltered to the output.

The Low-End Problem on Mix Buses

Low-frequency sounds carry significantly more acoustic energy than mid- and high-frequency sounds. On an unfiltered master bus or drum bus, a loud kick drum or deep sub-bass note will register as a massive volume spike in the compressor's detection circuit.

Without a sidechain filter, this energy forces the compressor to clamp down aggressively every time the kick or bass hits. As a result:

  • The mid- and high-frequency elements (vocals, guitars, cymbals) duck unnaturally in volume, creating unwanted "pumping."
  • The low end loses its dynamic punch, sounding flattened or suffocated.
  • The mix loses its perceived width and openness.

Key Functions and Benefits of Sidechain Filtering

  • Preserving Low-End Weight and Punch: By filtering the sub-bass out of the detector, the kick drum and bass guitar retain their natural impact without choking the rest of the mix.
  • Eliminating Unwanted Pumping: The compressor responds to the musical balance of the mid-range—where human hearing is most sensitive—rather than erratic sub-frequency bursts.
  • Creating a More Cohesive "Glue": Bus compression is primarily used to bind different instruments together. Sidechain filtering ensures the compression reacts to the groove and the vocal or snare balance, yielding smooth, transparent cohesion.

Typical Settings and Practical Application

Most modern bus compressors (both hardware and plugins) feature an internal sidechain high-pass filter with selectable frequencies, typically ranging from 60 Hz to 250 Hz.

  • 60 Hz to 90 Hz: Best for general master bus processing. It rolls off extreme sub-bass rumble from triggering the compressor while still allowing the core body of the kick drum to influence the dynamic movement.
  • 100 Hz to 160 Hz: Ideal for heavy, bass-driven modern genres such as hip-hop, electronic music, and modern rock. This range completely frees the low-end rhythm section from triggering gain reduction, allowing for aggressive bus compression focused purely on mid-range glue.
  • Above 200 Hz: Often useful on specialized groups, like an acoustic guitar bus or background vocal bus, to prevent low-mid buildup from suffocating high-frequency air and presence.
