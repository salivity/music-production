True Peak Metering to Prevent Inter-Sample Clipping

True peak metering is essential in modern audio production to ensure that exported audio remains free from distortion across all digital streaming platforms. While traditional digital meters measure individual discrete sample values, true peak meters detect the hidden analog peaks that emerge when those samples are reconstructed into continuous sound waves or compressed into lossy codecs. By accurately modeling the continuous waveform prior to digital-to-analog conversion, true peak metering allows mastering engineers to identify and prevent inter-sample clipping, safeguarding audio fidelity and meeting the strict technical standards of streaming services.

Traditional digital sample peak meters measure the amplitude of discrete audio samples at specific moments in time, displaying values relative to 0 dBFS (decibels full scale). If none of the individual samples exceed 0 dBFS, a standard meter will indicate no clipping. However, audio cannot be heard as discrete points; digital-to-analog converters (DACs) must smooth these points into a continuous analog waveform using reconstruction filters. During this interpolation, the curve between two adjacent samples—even if both are below 0 dBFS—can swing significantly higher than the samples themselves. This creates an "inter-sample peak" that exceeds 0 dBFS, driving the playback converter into analog clipping and introducing harsh harmonic distortion.

The risk of inter-sample clipping escalates dramatically during the delivery process for streaming music. Streaming services ingest uncompressed master files (such as WAVs) and transcode them into lossy formats like AAC, Ogg Vorbis, or MP3 to save bandwidth. The lossy encoding process discards perceptual audio data and alters the phase and amplitude relationships of the waveform. This transformation commonly causes peak levels to increase by 0.5 dB to 1.5 dB. When combined with unresolved inter-sample peaks, these transcoding-induced shifts virtually guarantee that a master normalized to 0 dBFS will distort on consumer playback systems.

True peak metering resolves this issue through the ITU-R BS.1770 standard. A true peak meter oversamples the incoming digital audio signal—typically by four or eight times the native sample rate—to approximate the continuous waveform created during digital-to-analog reconstruction. The meter measures this reconstructed signal in dBTP (decibels True Peak), revealing any inter-sample peaks that standard peak meters fail to detect.

By integrating true peak metering and true peak limiting into the export chain, engineers can actively contain inter-sample energy. The prevailing industry practice for streaming optimization is to set true peak limiters with a safety ceiling between -1.0 dBTP and -2.0 dBTP, especially for louder masters. This provides the necessary headroom to absorb both inter-sample interpolation swings and peak increases from lossy encoding, ensuring the audio retains its intended clarity and punch on any streaming service.
