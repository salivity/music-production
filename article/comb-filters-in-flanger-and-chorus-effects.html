Comb Filters in Flanger and Chorus Effects

Comb filtering is the fundamental acoustic and mathematical mechanism behind both flanger and chorus modulation effects in music production. This article explains how comb filters function, the role of short time delays and Low-Frequency Oscillators (LFOs) in shaping the audio spectrum, and the precise technical differences in how flangers and choruses utilize comb filtering to alter tone, depth, and movement.

A comb filter occurs when an audio signal is split, delayed by a minute interval, and then blended back with the original, unshifted signal. This time disparity causes constructive and destructive phase interference at regularly spaced intervals across the frequency spectrum. The resulting frequency response curve features a series of alternating sharp peaks (amplified frequencies) and deep notches (attenuated frequencies), visually resembling the teeth of a hair comb.

In flanger effects, the comb filter operates within a very short delay window, typically between 0.1 and 10 milliseconds. Because the delay is so brief, the human ear cannot perceive it as a distinct echo. Instead, the ear interprets the phase cancellations as a distinct tonal coloration. A flanger modulates this delay time continuously using an LFO. As the delay time shifts back and forth, the "teeth" of the comb filter slide up and down the frequency spectrum. This dynamic sweeping of harmonic notches produces the signature swooshing, jet-engine-like sound associated with flanging. Most flangers also incorporate a feedback loop—feeding the output back into the comb filter—which sharpens the peaks and deepens the notches, making the resonance significantly more pronounced.

In chorus effects, the comb filter operates with a longer delay time, generally ranging from 15 to 35 milliseconds. At this range, the comb filter’s notches are spaced much closer together across the spectrum. Rather than producing a dramatic, sweepable metallic notch, the modulation creates subtle pitch variations (via the Doppler effect) alongside the comb filtering. When an LFO modulates this slightly longer delay, the resulting time and pitch discrepancies mimic two or more separate sources playing the same part with minor timing imperfections. Unlike flanging, chorus rarely uses high feedback, focusing instead on softening phase notches to generate stereo width, warmth, and an ensemble-like thickness.

Ultimately, the comb filter serves as the core acoustic engine for both effects. By controlling the delay time, modulation rate, and feedback intensity of the comb filter, sound designers and producers can manipulate phase cancellation to transform static, single-source audio tracks into animated, wide, and richly textured elements within a mix.
