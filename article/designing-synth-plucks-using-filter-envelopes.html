Designing Synth Plucks Using Filter Envelopes

Synth plucks are an essential element in electronic music production, known for their sharp, percussive transients and short sonic tails. Producers craft these sounds primarily by using subtractive synthesis, routing harmonically rich waveforms through a low-pass filter modulated by an envelope with a fast attack and a short decay. This article breaks down the step-by-step process of designing punchy synth plucks, from oscillator selection to filter shaping, amplitude coordination, and post-processing.

1. Oscillator Selection and Waveforms

A pluck requires high-frequency content to give the filter something to sculpt. Producers typically begin with bright, harmonically dense waveforms:

  • Sawtooth Waves: Provide an aggressive, bright, and buzzy character, ideal for progressive house and trance plucks.
  • Square/Pulse Waves: Offer a hollow, woody tone, often favored in deep house and techno.
  • Wavetables: Modern wavetable synths allow for metallic, glassy, or acoustic-hybrid plucks.
  • Detuning and Unison: Adding 2 to 7 unison voices with slight detuning thickens the sound, creating width and richness.

2. Setting Up the Low-Pass Filter

The filter is the most critical component in defining the pluck's character.

  • Filter Type: A 24 dB/octave (4-pole) or 12 dB/octave (2-pole) low-pass filter is standard. A 24 dB slope creates a sharper, more defined snap, while a 12 dB slope sounds softer and more natural.
  • Cutoff Frequency: Turn the filter cutoff frequency almost all the way down. The resting state of the sound should be dull or nearly silent. The envelope will dynamically open this cutoff.

3. Shaping the Filter Envelope

The filter envelope dictates how quickly the filter opens and closes over time. This rapid movement generates the characteristic "pluck."

  • Attack (0 ms): The attack must be instantaneous so the filter snaps open the moment a MIDI note is triggered, generating a sharp transient.
  • Decay (100 ms to 400 ms): This is the defining parameter. A short decay time causes the filter to slam shut rapidly, cutting off high frequencies and producing the percussive effect. Shorter decay times create tighter, clicky plucks, while slightly longer times result in melodic, bouncy plucks.
  • Sustain (0%): Set the sustain level to zero. The filter should close completely rather than holding at an open frequency.
  • Release (0 ms to 200 ms): Keep the release short to match the decay, preventing notes from bleeding into one another when keys are released.
  • Envelope Amount (Modulation Depth): Increase the envelope amount to determine how high the cutoff opens. A higher depth results in a brighter, more pronounced transient.

4. Configuring the Amplitude Envelope

The amplitude (Amp) envelope shapes the volume of the sound and must complement the filter envelope:

  • Attack: 0 ms to ensure instant volume.
  • Decay: Set slightly longer than the filter decay. If the amp decay is too short, it will choke the sound before the filter finishes its sweep.
  • Sustain: Zero or very low to maintain the plucked behavior.
  • Release: Short, matching or slightly exceeding the filter release to avoid abrupt audio clicks.

5. Fine-Tuning Resonance and Key Tracking

  • Resonance: Adding a moderate amount of resonance emphasizes the frequencies right at the filter cutoff. This introduces a distinct "chirp" or "ping" at the start of the note. Pushing resonance too high can make the pluck sound overly harsh or piercing.
  • Key Tracking: Enable key tracking (100%) so the filter cutoff shifts relative to the note played on the keyboard. This ensures higher notes remain bright and lower notes retain their low-end punch without becoming muffled.

6. Effects and Mix Processing

Once the core patch is synthesized, producers use targeted effects to finish the sound:

  • Short Delays and Reverbs: Plucks naturally leave dynamic space in a mix. A ping-pong delay or an eighth-note delay, combined with a clean hall or plate reverb, adds spatial depth without cluttering the rhythm.
  • Compression: A compressor with a slow attack (10–30 ms) lets the initial transient pass through uncompressed before clamping down on the body, accentuating the punch.
  • Saturation and EQ: Gentle tape or tube saturation adds harmonic richness, while an EQ cuts unnecessary sub-frequencies below 100 Hz to leave room for the kick and bassline.
