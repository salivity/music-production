Audio Dithering: 24-Bit to 16-Bit Conversion

Audio dithering is a mathematical process applied during digital audio mastering to prevent harsh digital distortion when reducing an audio file's bit depth from 24-bit to 16-bit. By introducing a precisely calculated, microscopic amount of randomized noise to the signal before downsampling, dithering masks quantization errors and preserves low-level audio details like reverb tails and decaying notes. This article explains the mechanics of bit-depth reduction, the problem of truncation distortion, and how dithering ensures clean, professional 16-bit audio exports.

Understanding Bit Depth and Quantization

Bit depth dictates the dynamic range and amplitude resolution of digital audio. A 24-bit audio file possesses over 16.7 million discrete amplitude steps, offering approximately 144 dB of dynamic range. In contrast, standard 16-bit audio (the format used for standard CDs and some streaming deliverables) offers 65,536 amplitude steps, yielding roughly 96 dB of dynamic range.

When you render a 24-bit project down to a 16-bit file, 8 bits of data per sample must be removed. The audio engine must decide what to do with the extra data values that fall between the fewer steps available in 16-bit audio.

The Problem with Truncation

Without dithering, the digital audio workstation (DAW) drops the lowest 8 bits through a process called truncation. Truncation abruptly rounds or cuts off values to the nearest available 16-bit value.

Because this rounding error correlates directly with the musical waveform, it introduces quantization distortion. Unlike pleasant analog harmonic saturation, quantization distortion is unnatural, gritty, and discordant. It becomes most audible during low-volume passages, such as:

  • The natural fade-out of acoustic instruments
  • The quiet tails of reverbs and delays
  • Soft intros, outros, and silent gaps between tracks

At low amplitudes, truncation produces an audible, buzzy digital hash that ruins the clarity of the mix.

How Dithering Solves the Problem

Dithering prevents quantization distortion by introducing low-level, uncorrelated analog-like noise (typically Triangular Probability Density Function or TPDF noise) before the bit depth is reduced.

Although adding noise seems counterintuitive, this random signal decorrelates the rounding error from the audio signal. Instead of turning the discarded data into harsh, signal-dependent distortion, dithering transforms the error into a constant, steady, and benign hiss—similar to the tape hiss of analog recordings.

Crucially, dithering allows human ears to perceive audio details buried below the theoretical dynamic floor of the 16-bit format (extending perceptual dynamic range beyond 96 dB to around 115 dB to 120 dB). Reverb tails fade out smoothly into the noise floor rather than cutting off abruptly or crackling.

Noise Shaping

Modern dithering algorithms often include noise shaping. Noise shaping alters the frequency curve of the dither noise, pushing it out of the critical human hearing range (typically between 1 kHz and 5 kHz) and shifting it up toward higher, less audible frequencies (15 kHz to 20 kHz). This results in a perceptually quieter background noise floor while still eliminating quantization distortion.

Best Practices for Applying Dither

To maintain audio fidelity, apply dithering according to these rules:

  1. Dither only when downsampling bit depth: If you are exporting a 24-bit mix to a 16-bit master, apply dither. If you are rendering from 24-bit to 24-bit or 32-bit float, dithering is unnecessary.
  2. Apply dither as the very last step: The dither plugin must sit at the absolute end of the mastering chain, after all limiters, equalizers, and gain adjustments. Any processing applied after dither can alter the noise profile and reintroduce truncation artifacts.
  3. Dither only once: Avoid dithering at multiple stages of production. Repeated dithering stacks noise floors on top of each other, unnecessarily raising background noise without improving resolution.
