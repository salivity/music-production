How to Design Deep Sine Sub-Bass Patches

Designing deep, sustained sub-bass patches using pure sine waves requires a balance of precise synthesis, harmonic enhancement, envelope shaping, and low-end mixing. While pure sine waves provide the cleanest possible low-end fundamental, their lack of upper harmonics makes them difficult to hear on smaller speakers and challenging to control in dynamic arrangements. By tailoring the amplitude envelope, applying subtle saturation, shaping pitch dynamics, and isolating the patch in mono, music producers build powerful, consistent sub-bass tones that translate across all sound systems.

1. Oscillator Setup and Key Range

Start with a single oscillator initialized to a pure sine wave. Pure sine waves produce only the fundamental frequency with zero additional harmonics, giving you pure weight without sonic clutter.

  • Frequency Target: Keep the sub-bass within the 30 Hz to 65 Hz range (roughly C1 to C2). Frequencies below 30 Hz consume vast amounts of headroom without providing discernible pitch, while frequencies above 80 Hz enter the territory reserved for basslines, mid-basses, and kick drums.
  • Voicing: Set the synthesizer's voice mode strictly to Monophonic with Legato enabled. This prevents overlapping notes from generating phase cancellation or sudden volume spikes in the sub frequencies.

2. Amplitude Envelope (ADSR) Calibration

A sustained sub-bass must sustain flatly without clicking or muddying the transition between notes.

  • Attack (2–10 ms): A 0 ms attack causes the waveform to start abruptly off-zero, creating an audible, unwanted click. Introduce a tiny attack time (2 to 10 ms) to ensure the sine wave starts smoothly at a zero-crossing.
  • Decay and Sustain: Set the sustain level to 100% (0 dB) so the note maintains consistent energy throughout its duration.
  • Release (10–50 ms): Avoid a zero-release time, which causes clicks upon note release. A short release time smoothly ramps the amplitude down while preventing low-end bleed into the next note.

3. Adding Audible Harmonics

A pure sine wave is entirely inaudible on mobile devices, laptop speakers, and small earbuds. Producers introduce controlled harmonic distortion to generate higher overtones without destabilizing the fundamental frequency.

  • Soft Clipping and Tape Saturation: Drive the sine wave lightly into a tape saturator or soft clipper. This generates subtle second- and third-order harmonics (octaves and fifths above the fundamental), giving the sub presence in the 100 Hz to 250 Hz range.
  • Parallel Processing: To maintain absolute low-end purity, split the sub-bass into two channels: keep one channel as an untreated, clean sine wave filtered at 80 Hz, and saturate a duplicated channel filtered between 80 Hz and 300 Hz. Blend the saturated signal slightly behind the clean sub.

4. Pitch Envelopes and Portamento

For genres like Trap, Drum & Bass, and Dubstep, sustained subs require movement to stay engaging.

  • Attack Punch: Route a modulation envelope to the oscillator pitch with a decay of 10 to 30 ms and zero sustain. Dialing in a subtle pitch drop (between 1 and 7 semitones) gives the initial transient a percussive knock that cuts through a mix without conflicting with the kick drum.
  • Glide / Portamento: When writing sweeping 808-style subs, set the glide time between 50 ms and 150 ms. This allows continuous pitch slides that retain sustained energy without resetting the phase.

5. Mixing, Dynamics, and Phase Control

Low-end energy must be tightly managed to preserve headroom on master limiters and large sound systems.

  • Mono Enforcement: Ensure the sub-bass patch is 100% mono. Stereo information below 100 Hz causes destructive phase cancellation on club sound systems and vinyl cuts.
  • High-Pass Filtering: Apply a steep 24 dB/octave or 48 dB/octave high-pass filter around 25 Hz to 30 Hz to cut inaudible subsonic rumble that eats away dynamic headroom.
  • Kick-Sub Relationship: Carve out space for the kick drum using volume automation or fast sidechain compression. Pull down the sub-bass amplitude whenever the kick hits so both elements do not occupy the low-end simultaneously.
