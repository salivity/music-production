How Optical Compressors Use Light-Dependent Resistors

Optical compressors are legendary in music production for their musical, transparent dynamic control. This article examines the core mechanism behind these units: the optical cell. By converting an incoming audio signal into light and reading that light with a light-dependent resistor (LDR), optical compressors introduce natural physical latencies and non-linear release curves. Readers will learn how this unique electro-optical interaction shapes audio dynamics, why it prevents pumping artifacts, and how it delivers the smooth, forgiving compression heard on countless classic vocal and bass recordings.

The Basic Architecture of an Optical Cell

At the heart of any optical compressor (often referred to as an "opto" compressor) sits an enclosed, light-tight module containing two primary components: a light source and a light-sensitive detector.

  1. The Light Source: The incoming audio signal is fed into a sidechain circuit that drives a light emitter. In vintage units like the Teletronix LA-2A, this is an electroluminescent (T4) panel; in modern designs, it is often a specialized LED or incandescent bulb. As the audio signal gets louder, the voltage increases, causing the light source to glow brighter.
  2. The Light-Dependent Resistor (LDR): Positioned directly opposite the light source is a photoresistor (typically a Cadmium Sulfide, or CdS, photocell). The electrical resistance of this component changes relative to the amount of light hitting its surface.

How Gain Reduction Occurs

The LDR is wired into a voltage divider circuit within the main audio path. When no audio signal is present, the light source remains dark, and the LDR maintains high electrical resistance, allowing the audio signal to pass through unattenuated.

As the audio level exceeds the compressor's threshold, the light source illuminates the photocell. The light excites free electrons within the Cadmium Sulfide material, causing its electrical resistance to drop sharply. In a standard attenuation circuit, this drop in resistance diverts a portion of the main audio signal to ground. The brighter the light shines, the lower the resistance falls, and the more the audio signal is reduced.

Why LDRs Produce "Smooth" Dynamics

Unlike Voltage-Controlled Amplifier (VCA) or Field-Effect Transistor (FET) compressors—which rely on near-instantaneous silicon-based circuit switching—photocells rely on chemical and physical reactions. This physical response introduces several characteristics that sound exceptionally smooth to the human ear:

  • Non-Linear, Multi-Stage Release: Cadmium Sulfide cells exhibit a physical property known as "photoconductive memory" or photohysteresis. When the light turns off, the photocell does not immediately return to its dark resistance state. Instead, it drops quickly for the first 50% of the release, and then dramatically slows down for the remainder of the recovery. This two-stage release prevents the compressor from "pumping" or artificially modulating background noise.
  • Program-Dependent Response: The behavior of the LDR dynamically adapts to the incoming music. If a sound is brief (such as a single transient snare hit), the photocell recovers quickly because the light flash was brief. However, if the signal stays loud over a sustained period (such as a held vocal note or bass line), the photocell becomes saturated with light and takes substantially longer to recover.
  • Soft-Knee Characteristic: The relationship between the light intensity and the resistance change is non-linear and curved. As the signal approaches the compression threshold, gain reduction is applied gradually rather than abruptly. This eliminates the harsh, audible onset of compression common in faster, peak-limiting topologies.

Practical Impact in Music Production

Because light-dependent resistors cannot react instantly—typically featuring attack times between 1 and 10 milliseconds—they naturally preserve the initial, sharp transients of an instrument while transparently clamping down on the body of the sound. Combined with the multi-stage release curve, this makes optical compressors the industry standard for leveling vocals, smoothing out dynamic bass guitar performances, and adding cohesion to acoustic instruments without altering their fundamental tone or introducing distortion artifacts.
