What Is Audio Dithering in Digital Mastering?

Audio dithering is a mathematical process applied during the final stage of music mastering when reducing the bit depth of an audio file, such as converting a 24-bit or 32-bit floating-point master to a 16-bit file for CD or streaming distribution. By introducing a precisely calculated, low-level randomized noise, dithering eliminates harsh harmonic distortion caused by quantization errors and preserves low-amplitude details, reverbs, and fade-outs.

The Problem: Quantization and Truncation

Digital audio represents sound amplitude using discrete values determined by bit depth. A 24-bit recording provides over 16.7 million possible amplitude values, capturing extreme dynamic detail and very quiet signals. However, standard distribution formats often require lower bit depths, such as 16-bit, which offers only 65,536 values.

When downsampling word length without dithering, the processing algorithm simply chops off the excess digital information. This process is called truncation. Because the audio information between the remaining 16-bit levels is dropped, the system rounds sample values up or down to the nearest available step. This rounding is directly correlated with the music's waveform, resulting in quantization error. At low volumes—such as the tail of a reverb or an ending fade-out—this error manifests as harsh, gritty harmonic distortion that is perceptually fatiguing and unnatural to the human ear.

The Solution: How Dithering Works

Dithering prevents truncation distortion by decorrelating the rounding error from the audio signal. Before the file is reduced to the lower bit depth, the dithering algorithm injects an extremely quiet, microscopic bed of randomized analog-style noise into the signal.

This added noise causes the audio signal to constantly fluctuate across quantization boundaries, rather than snapping rigidly to the nearest bit value. Statistically, the lower-level information is preserved as variations in the timing and probability of these transitions. As a result, the harsh, signal-correlated distortion is converted into an inaudible, steady, broadband hiss, allowing the listener to perceive acoustic signals well below the theoretical noise floor of the target bit depth.

Types of Dithering Algorithms and Noise Shaping

Mastering engineers utilize different algorithmic curves depending on the sonic character of the material:

  • Flat Dither (TPDF): Triangular Probability Density Function (TPDF) adds an even distribution of noise across the entire audible frequency spectrum. It is mathematically neutral and transparent.
  • Noise-Shaped Dither: Advanced algorithms (such as POW-r, UV22HR, or MBIT+) use psychoacoustic curves to shift the dither noise away from frequencies where human ears are most sensitive (around 2 kHz to 5 kHz) into high-frequency regions (above 14 kHz to 18 kHz) where human hearing is weak. This maximizes dynamic range and reduces perceived noise.

Rules for Application

Dithering must only be applied once during the production lifecycle, serving as the final step on the master output bus. Applying dither multiple times stacks noise and degrades clarity. It should only be engaged when rendering audio to a lower bit depth than the working digital audio workstation (DAW) session; if the source and export bit depths are identical, dithering is not required.
